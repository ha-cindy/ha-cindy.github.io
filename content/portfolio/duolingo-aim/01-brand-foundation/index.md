## The question

Duolingo is huge (58M+ daily active users in Q2 2026), but its own numbers show a leak. Only about 12% of learners are still around at day 30. I wanted to find where that happens, who picks those learners up, and which group Duolingo should build for next.

## What I did

- **Mapped the competition.** I compared Babbel, Rosetta Stone, Preply, and Busuu on positioning, the customer each one wins, and where each one is weak.
- **Found the gap.** Duolingo already built a fix for the "I can't actually hold a conversation" problem: Video Call. But it's only in Max, which is about 9% of subscribers. Learners who hit that wall leave for paid tutors like Preply.
- **Segmented by motivation, not geography.** I split learners into Heritage and Identity, Academic and Career, and Casual Hobbyist, then wrote down why each group would pick Duolingo over the alternative.
- **Pressure-tested my own instinct.** I listed what supports a Gen Z focus and what complicates it (Millennials are actually the bigger U.S. generation, and low day-30 retention is a company-wide problem).

## What I found

The segment to commit to is **Gen Z heritage and identity learners**: young people using Duolingo to reconnect with family or culture. Heritage was the top reason new U.S. learners started in 2021, and Gen Z heritage learners spend more time studying than any other group measured. The opportunity is to move practical, real-conversation practice earlier in the free experience, since the first free lessons still lead with animal and food vocabulary instead of greeting your grandma.
